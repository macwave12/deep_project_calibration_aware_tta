\title{Agreement-Gated TDA: the cache that echoes CLIP, and a fix that adds no parameters}
\author{Gal Machlin (207627811) \and Idan Yehuda (207016270)}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
CLIP classifies images zero-shot, but its accuracy drops on data unlike its training images. TDA~\cite{karmanov2024tda}
adapts CLIP at test time with a cache of past test features labeled by CLIP's own predictions. It raises accuracy, but
leaves CLIP more overconfident. We show that much of this calibration loss is an \emph{echo}: the cache is filled with
CLIP's decisions, so on many samples it votes for the class CLIP already chose; such a vote cannot change the prediction
and only adds confidence. The fix follows: use the cache only where its top class differs from CLIP's. This
\emph{agreement gate} adds no parameters and costs one comparison per sample. On the test splits of five datasets with
CLIP ViT-B/16, TDA raises accuracy from \mainClipAcc\% to \mainTdaAcc\% but also the expected calibration error (ECE)
from \mainClipEce{} to \mainTdaEce; the gate reaches \mainGateAcc\% at an ECE of \mainGateEce. On \genAllN{} held-out
datasets the gate keeps TDA's accuracy, and on those far from CLIP's training images it lowers TDA's ECE on
\genFarEceWins{} of \genFarN, by up to \farBigHi{} points. Where its ECE is worse than TDA's, its errors lean toward
underconfidence, the safer side. Other label-free scores of CLIP and of the cache do not predict which of the cache's
changes to CLIP's decisions are right.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
CLIP~\cite{radford2021clip} classifies an image zero-shot by comparing its embedding with the embeddings of class
prompts (``a photo of a \{class\}''). Its accuracy depends on how close the test images are to its web training data:
on fine-grained or specialized datasets, such as textures, flowers, pet breeds, satellite images and aircraft models
(DTD, Flowers, Pets, EuroSAT, Aircraft; our \emph{design sets}), it reaches only \mainClipAcc\% on average.

\emph{Test-time adaptation} (TTA) improves a model on the test distribution while it is being tested, from the
unlabeled test inputs alone. We evaluate it by accuracy and by \emph{calibration}: whether the model's confidence, its
top softmax probability, matches how often it is right. Calibration is measured by the expected calibration
error~\cite{guo2017calibration}: the $N$ test samples are split into $M=20$ equal-width confidence bins
$B_1,\dots,B_M$, and
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{ECE} = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \frac{|B_m|}{N}\,\big|\mathrm{acc}(B_m) - \mathrm{conf}(B_m)\big|,
\label{eq:ece}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{acc}(B_m)$ and $\mathrm{conf}(B_m)$ are the accuracy and the mean confidence in bin $m$ (accuracy and ECE
are in \%). ECE does not say in which direction a model errs, so we also report its \emph{confidence--accuracy gap},
mean confidence minus accuracy: positive for an overconfident model, negative for an underconfident one. When confidence
decides whether a prediction is acted on, the two errors are not equally harmful: an underconfident model gives up some
predictions it could have kept, while an overconfident one acts on wrong predictions it trusts.

TDA~\cite{karmanov2024tda} (Fig.~\ref{fig:tda}) adapts CLIP online, one test sample at a time, with no labels and no
backpropagation, unlike prompt-tuning methods such as TPT~\cite{shu2022tpt} and its calibrated variant
C-TPT~\cite{yoon2024ctpt}. It keeps a cache of past test features labeled with CLIP's own predictions
(\emph{pseudo-labels}) and adds the votes of similar cached samples to CLIP's prediction. Like many TTA
methods~\cite{sheng2025illusion}, it raises accuracy and ECE together: on the design sets, from \mainClipAcc\% to
\mainTdaAcc\% and from \mainClipEce{} to \mainTdaEce{} (Table~\ref{tab:main}). We ask where this calibration loss comes
from, and remove it at its source.

\textbf{Contributions.} (1) A \emph{diagnosis} (\S\ref{sec:motivation}): TDA's cache agrees with CLIP on
\niAgreeShareLo--\niAgreeShareHi\% of the samples on average per tier (\niAgreeDsLo--\niAgreeDsHi\% per dataset). There
it cannot change the decision and can only raise confidence, which we prove; empirically, on the design sets it turns a
slight underconfidence on these samples into strong overconfidence.
(2) The \emph{agreement gate} (\S\ref{sec:method}): use the cache only where it disagrees with CLIP. (3) Evidence on
\totalDatasets{} datasets, \genAllN{} of them held out from the design, against TDA, post-hoc calibration and two other
training-free methods, with an ablation and a random control (\S\ref{sec:results}). (4) Its limits
(\S\ref{sec:limits}): when the gate helps, and why finer label-free scores do not improve on it.

\section{Background}\label{sec:background}
\textbf{CLIP.} For an image $x$ with normalized embedding $f(x)$, CLIP's logits over the $K$ classes are
$z_k(x) = \tau \langle f(x), t_k \rangle$, where $t_k$ is the normalized embedding of class $k$'s prompt and $\tau$ is
CLIP's logit scale. Its prediction, TDA's pseudo-label, is $\hat y_0(x) = \arg\max_k z_k(x)$.

\textbf{TDA.} The test samples arrive as a stream. For each class $k$, TDA keeps a \emph{positive cache} $\mathcal C_k$
of at most $S=3$ embeddings of past samples pseudo-labeled $k$, preferring low prediction entropy: a sample enters if its
class has room, or replaces the class's highest-entropy cached sample if its own entropy is lower. Each sample is first
written into the cache (if it enters) and then read. Reading gives, for each class, a similarity-weighted count of the
class's cached samples near $x$:
\begin{equation}
c_k(x) = \sum_{i \in \mathcal{C}_k} \exp\!\big(-\beta\,(1 - \langle f(x), f_i\rangle)\big) \in [0, S],
\qquad c(x) = \big(c_1(x), \dots, c_K(x)\big),
\label{eq:cache}
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ sets how fast a cached sample's weight decays with its distance to $x$. The class with the largest $c_k(x)$
is the \emph{cache's top class}. A \emph{negative cache}, built in the same way from uncertain samples, records classes
CLIP ruled out and gives a penalty $n(x)$ (see \citet{karmanov2024tda} for its thresholds). TDA's output logits are
\begin{equation}
o(x) = b(x) + \alpha\, c(x), \qquad b(x) = z(x) - n(x),
\label{eq:tda}
\end{equation}
so $b$ is CLIP's logits after the negative cache's correction, and $\alpha$ weighs the positive cache. TDA's paper sets
$\alpha = 2$, $\beta = 5$ once, on the ImageNet validation set, for all datasets. We use these values throughout, since
an online method should not need per-dataset tuning, and we do not evaluate on ImageNet itself. The per-dataset values
of TDA's code release do not do better here (Appendix~\ref{app:p1}).

\textbf{Post-hoc calibration.} The usual remedy for overconfidence is to divide the logits by a temperature, but
fitting it needs labels~\cite{guo2017calibration}, which TTA does not have. ZAEC~\cite{jiang2026zaec} is label-free: it
divides each adapted output by the smallest temperature $T(x) \ge 1$ that makes its entropy $H$ at least that of
zero-shot CLIP,
\begin{equation}
H\big(\mathrm{softmax}(o(x)/T(x))\big) \ge H\big(\mathrm{softmax}(z(x))\big).
\label{eq:zaec}
\end{equation}
Both treat the symptom after the fact. We look for its cause inside the cache.

\section{Diagnosis: the cache echoes CLIP}\label{sec:motivation}
This section locates TDA's calibration loss: where the cache agrees with CLIP, it adds confidence and nothing else. All
numbers in it come from the validation splits of the five design sets.

\textbf{The self-vote.} Because a sample is written before it is read, a sample that enters the cache finds itself
there: it adds exactly $1$ to $c_{\hat y_0}(x)$, more than any other cached sample can add. This vote adds nothing to
CLIP's own prediction, and it is frequent: \valSelfMin--\valSelfMax\% of the samples vote for themselves. Among samples
that vote for themselves, the cache's top class is CLIP's in \valAgreeSelfMin--\valAgreeSelfMax\% of cases; among the
others, in \valAgreeNoSelfMin--\valAgreeNoSelfMax\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:diag}a). Reading the cache before writing to it
(\emph{read-first TDA}) removes the vote and lowers TDA's ECE from \valTdaEce{} to \valNoSelfEce{}
(Fig.~\ref{fig:diag}b), at a small accuracy cost (\valTdaAcc\% $\to$ \valNoSelfAcc\%).

\textbf{Agreement in general.} Cached neighbors pseudo-labeled with CLIP's class make the cache agree with CLIP as well.
Call a sample \emph{agreeing} if the cache's top class is CLIP's, $\arg\max_k c_k(x) = \hat y_0(x)$, and
\emph{disagreeing} otherwise; we call the cache's vote for CLIP's own class the \emph{echo}.

\begin{lemma}[agreement]\label{lem:agree}
Let $\hat y = \arg\max_k b_k(x)$. If also $\hat y = \arg\max_k c_k(x)$, then $\arg\max_k o_k(x) = \hat y$, every margin
$o_{\hat y} - o_j$ is at least $b_{\hat y} - b_j$, and the top softmax probability of $o(x)$ is at least that of $b(x)$.
\end{lemma}
\noindent\emph{Proof.} $o_{\hat y} - o_j = (b_{\hat y} - b_j) + \alpha(c_{\hat y} - c_j) \ge b_{\hat y} - b_j > 0$, and
the top softmax probability $\big(1 + \sum_{j \ne \hat y} e^{-(o_{\hat y}-o_j)}\big)^{-1}$ increases with every margin.
$\square$

On agreeing samples the cache therefore cannot change the decision; it can only raise the confidence. (The lemma uses
the top class of $b$, which differs from CLIP's on \valAgreeNegMoved\% of the agreeing samples, where the negative cache
moved the decision.) The data match (Fig.~\ref{fig:diag}c,d): \valAgreeShare\% of the samples agree, and on them the
positive cache leaves accuracy unchanged (\valAgreeBaseAcc\% $\to$ \valAgreeTdaAcc\%) while it turns a slight
underconfidence into strong overconfidence (gap \valAgreeBaseGap{} $\to$ \valAgreeTdaGap{} points), far more confidence
than was missing. All of its accuracy gain comes from the disagreeing samples (\valDisagreeBaseAcc\% $\to$
\valDisagreeTdaAcc\%). The cache is useful exactly where it disagrees.

\section{Method: the agreement gate}\label{sec:method}
Use the positive cache only where its top class differs from CLIP's:
\begin{equation}
\tilde o(x) = b(x) + \alpha\, g(x)\, c(x), \qquad g(x) = \mathbf{1}\big[\arg\max_k c_k(x) \neq \hat y_0(x)\big].
\label{eq:gate}
\end{equation}
The gate is \emph{open} ($g = 1$) on disagreeing samples and closed on agreeing ones. It compares two decisions, not the
size of any score, since Lemma~\ref{lem:agree} depends only on which class wins in each. It compares with CLIP's class
$\hat y_0$ rather than with $b$'s because $\hat y_0$ is the label the cache is filled with, so agreement with it is the
echo itself. The caches, the admission rule, the negative cache, $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are TDA's, unchanged: the gate adds
no parameter, needs no label and costs one comparison per sample. A training-time precedent is
\citet{malach2017decoupling}, who update two networks trained on noisy labels only where their predictions disagree.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
We evaluate on three tiers of datasets. The \emph{design} tier is the five design sets: their validation splits were used
to design the method (\S\ref{sec:motivation}--\ref{sec:method}), which was fixed before any of its test or held-out
results were computed, and their test splits give the main result (\S\ref{sec:main}--\ref{sec:cmp}). The two
\emph{held-out} tiers played no part in the design and are defined by how far their images are from the web photos CLIP
was trained on (\S\ref{sec:gen}). On \emph{near} sets CLIP is already accurate and calibrated, so there is little to
adapt. On \emph{far} sets CLIP is weak, and test-time adaptation should matter most.

\textbf{Protocol.} CLIP ViT-B/16 with the CoOp splits~\cite{zhou2022coop}. TDA and the gate are online, so their output
depends on the order in which the test samples arrive. We run every method on the same random orders of each test set
(seeds: \mainSeeds{} for Table~\ref{tab:main}, 3 elsewhere) and report the mean. For Table~\ref{tab:main} we also give
90\% confidence intervals over seeds for the gate's differences from TDA: an interval that excludes zero means the
difference is unlikely to be due to the sample order (it says nothing about other datasets). CLIP features are extracted
once on a GPU, and TDA's stream is replayed on CPU from them (Appendix~\ref{app:p1}). Section~\ref{sec:motivation},
Tables~\ref{tab:comparison} and~\ref{tab:abl}, the $\alpha$ sweep of \S\ref{sec:abl} and the flip analysis of
\S\ref{sec:limits} re-evaluate logged runs of an equivalent implementation, whose design-set means agree with the
direct replay within \replayDiffMax{} points; Table~\ref{tab:abl} and the flip analysis leave out ImageNet-Sketch, of
which we logged only the first 500 samples.

\subsection{Main result}\label{sec:main}
On the mean over the five design test sets (Table~\ref{tab:main}), the gate lowers TDA's mean ECE from \mainTdaEce{} to
\mainGateEce{} (lower on all \mainSeeds{} seeds), slightly below CLIP's \mainClipEce. Its accuracy changes by \mainDAcc{} points (90\% CI

much better calibrated than TDA on DTD, Flowers, EuroSAT and Aircraft, and worse on Pets (\S\ref{sec:limits}).

\textbf{Near: little to adapt.} On these photos CLIP is already accurate (\genNearMeanClipAcc\%) and has a low ECE

and adaptation matters: TDA adds \genFarTdaGainAcc{} accuracy points, but its mean ECE rises from \genFarMeanClipEce{} to
\genFarMeanTdaEce. The gate keeps the accuracy gain (\genFarGateOverClipAcc{} over CLIP) and lowers TDA's ECE on
\genFarEceWins{} of the \genFarN{} sets, to \genFarMeanGateEce{} on average, below CLIP's. The improvement is uneven: on

has a lower ECE than TDA on \genAllEceWins{} (median change \genAllMedDEce{} points): where TDA does little, the gate does
little, and where TDA adapts, it keeps the adaptation.

Table~\ref{tab:abl} repeats the split of \S\ref{sec:motivation} on the design test sets and both held-out tiers. Added on

Is the gate just a smaller cache? Two controls say no. Turning down TDA's cache weight is not the same: at $\alpha = 0$
TDA's ECE falls to \sweepZeroEce, but its accuracy falls too, to \sweepZeroAcc\%; to reach the gate's accuracy
(\sweepGateAcc\%) TDA needs $\alpha \approx \sweepMatchAlpha$, where its ECE is \matchedTdaEce{} (design test sets). And
adding the cache on as many randomly chosen samples as the gate opens on is worse than the gate in both accuracy and ECE

(\ablRandAccFar\% / \ablRandEceFar{} against \ablGateAccFar\% / \ablGateEceFar); on near its extra confidence again lowers
ECE (\ablRandEceNear{} against \ablGateEceNear). Which samples the gate opens on matters, not only how many.

\label{fig:where}

\textbf{When does the gate help?} On agreeing samples the echo only adds confidence. Removing it should therefore help
where CLIP is calibrated or overconfident, and hurt where CLIP is underconfident, since there the added confidence
happens to correct it. Across the \rhoN{} design, near and far sets, the more overconfident CLIP is, the more the gate
lowers ECE (Fig.~\ref{fig:where}; Spearman $\rho = \rhoGapAll$; \rhoGapTest{} within the design sets and \rhoGapFar{}
within far, while near has only three sets). It is a tendency, not a rule: GTSRB loses a little although CLIP is not
underconfident there, and Cars gains although CLIP is mildly underconfident there. The gap needs labels, so it explains
the gate's behavior but cannot drive it; a label-free signal, CLIP's mean confidence on the first \signalHead{} samples
of the stream, is related to it only weakly and inconsistently ($\rho = \rhoConfAll$ over all \rhoN{} sets,
\rhoConfFar{} within far).

\textbf{Over- or underconfident?} The gap shows the direction of each error. TDA errs toward overconfidence: over the
\gapN{} design, near and far sets it raises CLIP's gap on \gapTdaUpN{} and is overconfident on \gapTdaOverN, while the
gate lowers TDA's gap on all \gapGateDownN{} (Table~\ref{tab:gen} gives the held-out sets). The mean gap is
\gapTestClip{} for CLIP, \gapTestTda{} for TDA and \gapTestGate{} for the gate on the design test sets, and
\gapFarClip, \gapFarTda{} and \gapFarGate{} on far. On near, where the three have about the same ECE, they err in
opposite directions: TDA is overconfident (\gapNearTda), CLIP and the gate underconfident (\gapNearClip, \gapNearGate).
TDA has a lower ECE than the gate on \gapLoseN{} sets. On Pets, Caltech101 and ImageNet-R, CLIP is underconfident and
TDA's extra confidence lands closer to the accuracy; on Food101 the two are about equal; on GTSRB the gate's mean
confidence matches its accuracy, but its over- and underconfident bins, which cancel in the gap, add up in ECE. On all
five the gate is underconfident or, on GTSRB, calibrated in the mean: where it loses, it errs on the safer side.

\textbf{Have CLIP and the cache given what they can?} The gate reads one bit, agreement. TDA's accuracy gain comes from
\emph{flips}, samples on which its decision differs from CLIP's. A flip is right if TDA is right and CLIP wrong, and wrong
if CLIP is right and TDA wrong; flips on which both are wrong are left out, since reverting them cannot change accuracy.
Reverting a set of flips to CLIP's decision therefore raises accuracy only if fewer than half of them are right.
Reverting exactly the wrong flips would add \niOracleBlockLo--\niOracleBlockHi{} accuracy points per tier, so a finer
control of the cache could still gain, if a label-free score told right flips from wrong ones.

We test one score of each kind on the held-out flips: CLIP's confidence, the cache's confidence (the largest share of
$c(x)$), and their similarity ($1 -$ the Jensen--Shannon divergence between their class distributions). Within a
dataset, the Spearman correlation between a flip being right and the score is small on average: its mean over a tier's
datasets is at most \niCorrMaxAbs{} in magnitude for any of the three (though \niCorrDsMaxAbs{} on one dataset), and for
the cache's confidence it is \niCorrCacheNear{} on near but \niCorrCacheFar{} on far. The share of right
flips stays at about one half or more in every decile of every score (Fig.~\ref{fig:noinfo}; lowest
\niDecileMinNear\% on near and \niDecileMinFar\% on far), so reverting the flips in any range of a score cannot raise
accuracy by much. Seven further scores, threshold rules chosen on validation flips, and a regression combining all ten
do no better (Appendix~\ref{app:flips}).

CLIP and the cache, as they are, seem to have given what they can: the cache helps where it disagrees with CLIP, and
which of its flips are right is not visible in the scores of either. Choosing which flips to keep likely needs another
source of information, such as augmented views or a second model.

\textbf{Limitations.} (i) Where CLIP is underconfident, the gate usually loses to TDA in ECE (on \gapLoseUnderN{} of the \gapUnderN{} such
sets; not on Cars), though on the safer side. (ii) Where
the gate is open, predictions stay overconfident on the hardest sets (e.g.\ DTD); ZAEC on top lowers ECE further
(Table~\ref{tab:comparison}). (iii) One backbone (ViT-B/16), and the gate applied to one cache method; the argument
applies to any cache filled with the model's own pseudo-labels, such as DMN's. (iv) Before the diagnosis we evaluated
other changes to TDA's cache on the test splits (stricter admission, probability fusion, a leave-one-out temperature),
all negative or partial; the gate itself was then designed on the validation splits, and the held-out sets played no
part in it. (v) The near tier has only three sets.

\section{Conclusion}
TDA's positive cache often only echoes CLIP: where it agrees with CLIP, it cannot change the decision and only adds
confidence. Gating it on disagreement keeps TDA's accuracy and brings ECE back to CLIP's level on the design sets; on
held-out data far from CLIP's training images, where TDA's calibration loss can be large, it removes most of it. Its cost
is mostly confined to data on which CLIP is underconfident, and there it errs on the safer side. Telling such data apart
without labels is the open problem.

\section{Flip analysis: all ten scores}\label{app:flips}
The ten label-free scores are three of CLIP (its confidence, its top-1 $-$ top-2 margin, its probability of the cache's
top class), three of the cache (its confidence, i.e.\ the largest share of $c(x)$; the mass $c_k(x)$ of its top class;
its share on CLIP's class) and four of their relation (the correlation of $c(x)$ and $z(x)$ over classes; $1 -$ their
Jensen--Shannon divergence; the cache's share on CLIP's class minus that on its own top class; the $\alpha$ at which the
cache would flip CLIP's decision). Fig.~\ref{fig:scores} shows the share of right flips along each, on the held-out
tiers. On near a few deciles dip just below one half (lowest \niDecileAllMinNear\%), on far none does (lowest
\niDecileAllMinFar\%). For each score, the best rule ``revert the flips below (or above) a threshold'', with the side and
the threshold (between the 5th and 95th percentile of the validation scores, so that some flips are reverted) chosen on
the validation flips, loses accuracy even on validation (at best \niRuleBestVal{} points) and gains nothing on the
held-out tiers (at best \niRuleBestNear{} on near and \niRuleBestFar{} on far). All ten combined in a logistic regression fitted on the validation
flips (reverting a flip when its predicted probability of being right is below \niThresh) gains \niFilterUVal{} points
on validation and loses up to \niFilterMax{} on far. Its AUC (the probability that a random right flip scores above a
random wrong one) is \niCombAucVal{} on the validation flips it was fitted on and \niCombAucTest{} on the design test
splits (same datasets), but \niCombAucNear{} on near and \niCombAucFar{} on far: it learns something specific to the
design datasets, not which flips are right.
